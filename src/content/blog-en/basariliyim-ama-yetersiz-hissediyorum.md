“The story we tell ourselves is often stronger than the results.” This sounds familiar to many people who feel inadequate despite being successful. From the outside, there is a good job, responsibilities accepted, completed projects or respected results. Inside, another voice says: I am not really that good. I was lucky. Soon they will find out.

This feeling is often neither laziness nor ingratitude. A person may have worked hard but struggle to give themselves credit. They attribute good outcomes to circumstances, help or luck, while treating mistakes as evidence of their character. One success brings temporary relief; then the bar rises again. An inner sense of competence never quite arrives.

The coaching app helps you notice the language you use to assess yourself. How do I explain success? How do I interpret mistakes? What would I say to someone else who achieved the same result? Why do I not offer myself the same fairness? These questions can make the beliefs beneath inadequacy more visible.

Feeling inadequate despite success is also often connected to fear of visibility. A person may fear that more responsibility will raise expectations, that a mistake will reduce their worth or that others will decide they are overrated. Without noticing, they may turn down opportunities or make themselves seem smaller than they are.

Perda's purpose is not to manufacture confidence. General statements such as “You are amazing” may feel good briefly without addressing the deeper doubt. A session helps you see your effort more realistically and hold both your strengths and your areas for development in view.

Solid confidence does not mean never making mistakes. It means knowing a mistake does not erase your worth. Accepting an achievement without diminishing it and learning from a shortcoming without tearing yourself down creates a more balanced inner foundation.

As that foundation develops, success can stop feeling like something that must constantly be proved. You can own your effort while remaining open to growth. Seeing that both are possible can soften feelings of inadequacy.
