“Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space lies our power to choose. In our choice lie our growth and freedom.” — Viktor E. Frankl

Other people may influence many of the things that happen to us. Ignoring that would be unrealistic. But keeping all attention outside ourselves can also mean placing our power outside ourselves. The victim role may bring short-term comfort by lightening the burden of responsibility. Saying “I am this way because of what they did” can offer temporary relief. In the longer term, however, it can obscure what is within our control and reinforce the feeling of being stuck.

Others have undoubtedly played a part in many events. The question is where our attention remains. Turning towards what is within our control can reveal options we had not considered. This means reclaiming our influence, without excusing the other person's actions.

The coaching app helps you reorganise your focus without minimising what happened. Its questions make “Where does my influence begin?” visible, rather than staying with “Who is to blame?” Instead of imposing a solution, it gives you room to use your own capacity to find one. The psychological concept of locus of control highlights a similar distinction: focusing only on what cannot be controlled can increase helplessness, while seeing small, realistic areas of influence can open room for action.

You do not have to stop noticing what others did. You can return to your own next step instead of remaining there. That might mean setting a boundary, opening a conversation or redefining an expectation. Power can lie in a small choice you own rather than a grand plan for revenge.

The aim goes beyond generic suggestions to “think positively”. It helps you remember your sphere of influence while acknowledging reality. Seeing your next step can reduce helplessness because you feel like an active participant in your story. That feeling can open new possibilities.

This shift need not happen overnight. Even a small act of ownership can begin to change “I cannot do anything” into “For now, I could try this.”

Moving beyond the victim role does not minimise pain. It acknowledges that pain while looking for a next step without becoming trapped within it. Sometimes a person focuses so intensely on proving they are right that they cannot see what being right gives them or costs them. That feeling may offer temporary strength, but without room for action, they can remain in the same place.

The coaching app invites you towards responsibility without blame. Alongside “What could you not control in this event?”, it asks “What is the smallest thing you can control from now on?” What helps is recognising today's influence rather than rewriting the past. That influence may be as small as changing one sentence, clarifying a boundary or naming an expectation differently.

When this small area becomes visible, feelings may begin to shift. Anger, helplessness or resentment do not instantly disappear, but a new possibility of movement emerges. This is the beginning of solution-focused thinking. Instead of surface-level advice such as “Don't worry about it” or “Move on”, Perda helps you see a choice while working through your feelings.

Moving beyond the victim role is a more mature form of ownership rather than harsh self-criticism. As you approach “What can I do for myself from now on?” without becoming stuck on “What did they do to me?”, your story can begin to change direction.
